# Part 1: How it works

## You are in an adult class now

This is a seminar, not a lecture course. Nobody's going to walk you through this step by step — expect a demo once or twice each session, and the rest of the time it's on you and your team. That's not a lack of attention; it's how a class works when everyone in it is trusted to run their own project.

Each of you is building your own robot. But you are not four separate projects that happen to share a room — you're a team, and the more experienced builders are expected to bring the newest member along. If that's you, ask. If you're one of the experienced three, notice when someone's stuck before they have to ask.

## What a squarebot actually is

A squarebot is the simplest robot chassis there is: a square (or rectangular) frame, wheels, and motors — nothing else. No arms, no lifts, no sensors doing anything fancy yet. It exists so that every later project (movement, controller, autonomous driving) has something solid to run on. Simple on purpose.

Your squarebot uses **four mecanum wheels** — two driven directly by the PRIZM controller's built-in motor ports, and two driven by a PRIZM DC Expansion module chained onto the controller over I2C. That's a real hardware constraint, not a design choice: the PRIZM controller only has two motor ports of its own, so a four-wheel drivetrain needs the expansion module for the other two. (If your kit is actually a simpler two-motor tank drive instead, the same design process applies — you'll just skip the expansion module and mount two wheels instead of four.)

## Why design before you build

It's tempting to skip straight to snapping parts together. Two reasons not to:

- **Mistakes are free in OnShape and expensive in real parts.** If your motor mounts collide with your wheels in the model, you find out by looking at a screen. If you find out by trying to bolt it together, you've already spent the time.
- **This is a real engineering skill, not busywork.** Every robotics team that builds anything nontrivial designs before it builds. You're doing the same thing here on a small scale.

## Using the class TETRIX library

You are not modeling parts from scratch. There's a TETRIX component library already set up in OnShape for this class — rails, motor mounts, wheels, all as ready-to-use parts. Your job is to pull the right parts in and assemble them correctly, not to draw a rail from a blank sketch. That's a real skill on its own: picking the right part, positioning it, and mating it so it actually behaves the way it will in real life.

A **mate** in OnShape is what tells two parts how they're allowed to move relative to each other — bolted rigidly in place, or free to spin like a wheel on an axle. Get the mates wrong and the model will look right sitting still but won't move the way your real robot needs to.

## What "correct" looks like for a squarebot

- **Square.** The frame's rails should form an actual square or rectangle — not a rhombus, not something with one side longer than the other by accident.
- **Wheels aligned and evenly spaced.** All four wheels touching the same reference plane, spaced symmetrically left-right and front-back.
- **Motors oriented so the drivetrain will track straight.** A motor mounted backwards on one side will drive that wheel the wrong direction.
- **No interference.** Nothing overlapping, nothing that would physically collide once real bolts and real wires are in the way.
- **Complete.** The full assembly — rails, motor mounts, wheels, and mounting points for your battery and PRIZM controller/expansion module — not just the frame by itself.

# Part 2: Do the work

## Step 1: Open the class library

Open OnShape and find the shared TETRIX library document you've been given access to for this class. Don't copy parts out of it into a random new document from scratch — insert them directly into your own assembly so you're always working with the real, correctly-dimensioned parts.

## Step 2: Build the frame

Pull in rails and construct a square (or rectangular) base. Mate them rigidly — this frame doesn't move relative to itself.

## Step 3: Mount the motors and wheels

Add motor mounts at the four corners (or wherever your frame design puts them) and attach a mecanum wheel to each motor. Double-check that mecanum wheels are oriented correctly — they come in left-handed and right-handed pairs, and getting this wrong is the single most common mecanum mistake. Mate each wheel so it can rotate freely about its own axle; that's the one place in this whole assembly where a part should NOT be rigid.

## Step 4: Mount the controller and expansion module

Add mounting points for your PRIZM controller and, since this is a four-wheel mecanum drivetrain, your I2C DC expansion module. Leave room for wiring — the Powerpole motor connectors and I2C daisy-chain cable both need physical space to route without stretching or pinching.

## Step 5: Mount the battery

TETRIX PRIZM runs on the 12V rechargeable NiMH battery pack — nothing else. Give it a real mounting location in your model, not just floating space near the frame. Weight distribution matters once this thing is actually driving.

## Step 6: Check your own work before you demo it

Before you demo it:

- Rotate the assembly and look for anything overlapping.
- Spin each wheel (drag it, if OnShape lets you, or just check the mate type) and confirm it's actually free to rotate.
- Step back and ask: does this look square? Symmetric? Would you trust it to roll straight?
