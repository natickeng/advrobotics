# Part 1: How it works

## Why you're writing this natively

Your PRIZM controller is built around an 8-bit AVR chip that mirrors an Arduino Uno, programmed in C++ through the Arduino IDE — a small, simple computer. Autonomous movement here gets written directly, using the same PRIZM API you've already been using for movement and controller code, not an off-the-shelf path-following package.

That's not a limitation to work around. A former student built a PRIZM robot that followed a bezier curve using nothing but C++ and this exact class of hardware. This project's bar is a lot lower than that, but the ceiling is genuinely there if you want to push it for the flex.

## Encoders make this real autonomous, not guessing

Your PRIZM controller hardwires Motor Port 1 to Encoder Port 1, and Motor Port 2 to Encoder Port 2 — that's fixed, not configurable. That means your two front wheels have real position/speed feedback and your back two (if you're on a four-wheel mecanum drivetrain, driven through an I2C expansion module) don't.

That's not actually a problem for straight-line or fixed-turn autonomous movement: as long as your robot isn't rotating its chassis while it moves, your back-left wheel always wants the same speed as your front-right wheel, and your back-right always wants the same speed as your front-left, by simple mecanum geometry. So closing the loop on your two front encoders and mirroring those corrected values to the back two gets you real closed-loop control on all four wheels, using encoders you only have on two of them.

Closed-loop, in plain terms: measure how fast a wheel is actually turning (from the encoder), compare it to how fast you wanted it to turn, and adjust the commanded power to close the gap. Without this, your robot's actual speed drifts with battery charge, floor friction, and which specific motor you happen to have — open-loop autonomous code that worked perfectly on a full battery can miss badly on a half-drained one.

## Direct Ollama with this in mind

Tell it: you have encoder feedback on two motors only, the other two mirror them, and you want closed-loop speed correction, not just open-loop power-and-time. That's enough for it to write something reasonable — you don't need to hand it a control-theory lecture, just the real constraint.

# Part 2: Do the work

## Step 1: Read your encoders

Before writing any autonomous behavior, confirm you can read encoder counts from your two front motors and that the numbers change sensibly when you spin the wheels by hand or run a motor.

## Step 2: Closed-loop straight-line movement

Direct Ollama to write code that drives forward a target distance, using encoder feedback to correct wheel speed as it goes, and stops — no controller involved. Compare it against your Project 03 open-loop version: does it hold a straighter line, and does it behave more consistently run to run?

## Step 3: Add a turn and a second leg

Extend the program to execute at least two movement legs with a turn between them — for example, drive forward, turn, drive forward again. This is the minimum shape of a "path": more than one instruction, executed without a human in the loop.

## The test (checked at your demo)

Run your finished autonomous program once, live, with nobody touching a controller. It passes if:

1. The robot completes a multi-leg path (at least two straight legs and a turn) entirely on its own.
2. It stops cleanly at the end — no coasting, no needing to be caught.
3. Run it twice. The two runs should look close to identical — that repeatability is what closing the loop with encoders is actually for. If the two runs look wildly different, the closed-loop correction isn't doing its job yet.
